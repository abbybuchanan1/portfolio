Research practice

How I do research

What I ask of participants, and what I make sure flows back.

Abby BuchananNovember 2026

Research asks something of people: their time, their stories, sometimes their trust. I try to design my studies so that what flows back is as deliberate as what I ask.

Research should give something back. I decide at the start what participants get from a study, not only what I get from them. Every person I interview receives a plain-language summary of what I learned.

Consent is a conversation, not a form. I ask for permission to record three times: when I first reach out, when someone books, and out loud before I press record. Anyone can skip a question or stop at any point, and nothing changes for them.

People decide how they’re described. I describe participants by broad roles, never names or details that point to one person. At the end of each session I ask, “Is it OK if I describe you as ___?” and use their answer.

I don’t make people translate themselves. Much of my work is about the effort people spend reshaping what they mean so a system will accept it. I try not to impose that effort in my own research. People can take part by call, in writing, or by voice memo, and I use their words in my findings rather than mine.

Time is paid for, or the exchange is honest. When a project has a budget, I pay participants for their time. When it doesn’t, I say so up front, keep sessions short, and make sure the summary is worth having.

I collect only what I need, and let it go. I keep recordings only as long as a study runs, then delete them. I don’t collect details I won’t use.

Interpretation is shared work. Where I can, I show participants how I’ve described their experience before I publish it, and I correct what I got wrong.

Access is part of the method. I ask every participant what would help them take part comfortably, such as captions, extra time or a different format, and I plan sessions around the answer.

Where this comes from

These practices grew from my own work on how systems distribute effort and benefit. I’ve been shaped by scholars who have thought about this far longer: Linda Tuhiwai Smith’s account of how research has extracted from the communities it studies (Decolonizing Methodologies, 3rd edition, 2021), and Sasha Costanza-Chock’s case for design led by the people most affected by it (Design Justice, MIT Press, 2020, open access).
