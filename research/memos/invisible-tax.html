Research Memo 01

The Invisible Tax

How systems mistake compatibility for competence

Abby BuchananJuly 2026 · Updated November 2026Working memo · Provisional
Abstract

Knowledge work runs on a layer of labor that rarely gets named: translating what people think and want into forms a system can recognize. This memo looks at how that hidden work shapes who seems competent, who gets access, and whose ideas are heard.

A lot of people think the hard part of knowledge work is having the idea. Often it isn’t. The hard part is turning the idea into something a system can recognize: a thought into a slide deck, a pattern into a report, an instinct into a clear explanation in the right tone and order, in a format someone else can scan and approve. That translation takes a lot of energy, and most of the time nobody counts it.

We tend to judge work by what we can see: the presentation, the summary, the ticket. Those things usually aren’t the thinking itself. They’re evidence that someone managed to translate their thinking into a form the system could take in.

Some people do that translation easily. Others find it costly, and that says less about their insight than about the kind of fluency a system rewards. If your thinking is linear and structured, you’re more likely to be seen as clear and strategic. If it’s associative, intuitive, or hard to flatten into bullet points on demand, the same intelligence can be much harder for a system to see. A lot of what gets read as competence is really compatibility. I call the cost of that translation the invisible tax.

You can see the tax in the effort it takes to write something that sounds “professional,” or to reorganize a rich, messy idea into something polished and defensible. For some people that cost is small. For others it’s enormous, and they pay it on almost everything they contribute.

Access sets the price. In some workplaces ideas travel through relationships: conversation, trust, reputation. Some people can mention a thought in passing and have it taken seriously. Others aren’t in those rooms, so the formal system is their only way to be heard. Their idea has to be written down, organized, justified, and shaped to stand on its own. Some people rarely pass through the translation layer, and others pass through it every time.

The same thing happens to knowledge, not only to people. In my research on how ecological landscapes get built, a senior policy analyst described how project teams reliably design around what the rules name, like wetlands, and miss what they don’t: a 250-year-old oak, or a sunny slope where turtles nest. That knowledge is real, but it isn’t in a form the process recognizes, so it arrives late, after the design is paid for, or not at all. There, the cost of translation falls on the land itself.

This is one reason I’m so interested in AI. A surprising amount of what AI does well is translation: structuring text, summarizing, reorganizing ideas, adjusting tone. If it can carry some of that load, more people may be able to contribute in ways friction used to block, and ideas that never made it into the room may finally show up.

The opposite is possible too. If organizations respond by raising the bar, expecting more polish, more output, and faster replies, the tax doesn’t go away. It resets at a higher level.

So the question I care about is less whether AI is good or bad, and more whether it changes whose thinking gets to count. Most systems aren’t built to recognize intelligence in all its forms. They’re built to recognize what’s familiar and fluent. Making tools more efficient is part of the answer. The bigger part is designing systems that ask less translation of people in the first place.

Evidence status

This memo is an exploratory argument, not a completed study. It develops questions and distinctions for further research; factual and causal claims need independent sourcing before they’re treated as findings. The example of the senior policy analyst comes from my November 2026 field brief on landscape implementation.
