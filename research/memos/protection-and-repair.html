Research Memo 06

Protection and Repair

Why systems can stay unwell after a threat has passed, and what helps them recover

Abby BuchananNovember 2026Working memo · Provisional
Abstract

Memo 05 described how growth goes wrong when a system’s four functions (boundaries, resource flow, feedback and differentiation) are captured or suppressed. This memo is about what comes after: why a system can stay unwell once the threat has passed, and what recovery actually takes. Three patterns recur. Harm often comes from something in the wrong place, not something bad in itself. Protection can outlast the threat and become the harm. And the people responsible for protecting a system can come to depend on the problem continuing. When repair works, it restores a system’s self-correction across several functions at once, costs most at the start, and is usually judged too early.

A threat can pass and a system can still stay unwell.

Where this picks up

Memo 05 ends with a claim: repair is not reversal. If harm comes from a system losing its ability to correct itself, recovery means restoring that ability, not swapping who is in charge. I use the terms as Memo 05 defines them: growth by extraction, growth by development, and self-correction, meaning a system’s own ability to notice harm and adjust, not rules or permits imposed from outside.

This memo follows that claim further. I’ve kept mostly to two kinds of system: bodies, and places people design and then have to look after.

Harm from misplacement, not malice

Much harm comes from something in the wrong place, at the wrong scale or at the wrong time, not from something bad in itself. Bacteria that help digestion in one part of the gut can cause trouble in another (StatPearls, n.d.). A plant that holds a riverbank together in its native range can crowd out everything around it on another continent. A rule written to protect one kind of site can block good work on another.

This changes what repair looks for. If the thing itself is the problem, the answer is to remove it. If placement is the problem, the answer may be to move it, contain it, or restore the conditions that used to keep it in balance. The second question is usually the more useful one to ask first.

Protection that won’t stand down

A protective response is meant to switch on, deal with a threat, and switch off. Sometimes it doesn’t switch off.

In a body, a stress response that stays on pulls resources away from upkeep. Digestion slows, repair is put off, and the system runs on emergency settings it can’t sustain (Browning and Travagli, 2014). In autoimmune conditions, protection turns inward and the body’s defenses attack its own tissue, sometimes because something of the body’s own resembles a threat it has met before. This is called molecular mimicry, and on its own it rarely causes autoimmunity (Martins et al., 2023).

In institutions, the same pattern shows up as emergency measures that never lapse, review processes so cautious they block the projects they were meant to protect, and controls that treat anything new as a threat.

This is not weakness, and it isn’t malice. It is usually memory: protection calibrated to a real past harm and never re-tuned. That is why blaming the protector rarely helps. What helps is giving the system a safe way to stand down, and a signal it trusts that the threat has passed.

When protectors depend on the threat

There’s a second reason protection persists, and it has to do with incentives. When each team’s budget, jobs or standing rests on its own problem, no team gains from the whole system recovering. A win for one can be an obstacle for another. The writer Clay Shirky put a version of this in one line in 2010: institutions will try to preserve the problem to which they are the solution (Kelly, 2010). It doesn’t take bad intent. It’s what happens when people are rewarded for managing a problem rather than ending it.

Think of everyone who intervenes on a project after the plan is approved: specialists, contractors, reviewers, maintenance crews. Each is judged by their own measure. A crew may be paid per visit, a reviewer judged on risks avoided, a consultant paid per report: each acts sensibly, and together they can keep a system dependent on them.

The answer isn’t to remove the protectors. It’s to design the work so that protectors succeed when the whole system recovers: contracts, fees and measures tied to the system’s health over time, and a clear, respected role for people whose work becomes less needed.

Repair is not reversal

If distortion comes from a system losing its ability to correct itself, repair means restoring that ability, not simply reversing who holds power. A system that only swaps who is on top keeps the same extractive logic with new people running it.

Repair works on the functions themselves:

  1. Clarify boundaries.
  2. Restore signals, so harm is noticed and heard.
  3. Rebalance resource flows.
  4. Correct incentives that reward extraction, or that reward managing a problem over ending it.
  5. Let parts differentiate again.
  6. Strengthen self-correction, so the system can recover on its own next time.

Three things seem to hold for how that work goes.

It takes several parts at once. Recovery rarely comes from one intervention. Clearing an overgrowth (boundaries), restoring movement (flow), replenishing what was depleted (resources) and calming an over-alert system (feedback) each help, and each tends to fail alone. The parts reinforce each other, so their order and timing matter as much as the parts themselves.

When the usual channel is blocked, go direct. Sometimes a resource can’t reach where it’s needed because the channel that normally carries it is the thing that’s failing. A body that can’t absorb a nutrient through the gut can receive it directly into the blood (Kumar and Brookes, 2020). An organization whose usual channel is captured or overloaded may need a direct route while the channel is repaired, not after.

It looks worse before it gets better. Repair has a cost curve, and the cost comes first. Gardeners have a saying for perennials: the first year they sleep, the second year they creep, the third year they leap (American Meadows, n.d.). In the first year, most of the growth is underground, where no one sees it. A system judged during those years can look like it’s failing when it’s actually taking root. Many good plans are abandoned in the gap between the cost and the visible result.

A farm as a case

The documentary The Biggest Little Farm (2019) follows John and Molly Chester’s years-long effort to turn worn-out land in California into a diverse working farm. At first, each pest got its own fix. Things changed when they started encouraging relationships between species and recognizing that a healthy farm would require each one finding their own place rather than trying to treat “pests” in isolation, or even regarding them as pests in the first place.

But it was not as idyllic as it sounds. Initially some fixes caused the next problem. The ground cover they grew between the fruit trees held moisture and rebuilt the soil, but it also sheltered thousands of snails that stripped the leaves from the citrus, and it drew what John Chester called “the worst gopher problem in probably Ventura County” (Fresh Air, 2019). Meanwhile, in a drought, the ducks’ droppings were turning their pond toxic. Moving the ducks out of the pond and into the orchard turned one problem into the answer to another: the ducks ate the snails. Neither the snails nor the ducks were the problem in themselves. Each was in the wrong place.

What I take from it: self-correction spread across many relationships held up better than control from one point. The early years were the hardest and looked the least successful. And the farmers’ role shifted from fighting each problem to arranging the conditions for the system to correct itself. It’s one farm, told by the people who ran it, so I hold it as an illustration, not evidence.

What this is not

  1. Illness is not a moral failure. Infection is not evil. Cancer is not immoral. Disease is not punishment. These are system states. That holds for people living with illness and for every system this frame describes.
  2. Protectors are not villains. Protection that won’t stand down is usually doing what it learned to do. The aim is to re-tune it, not to blame it.
  3. Not against intervention. Some threats are real, and some protection has to stay on. The question is whether it can still stand down once the threat passes.
  4. No guarantee. Repair can fail even when it’s done well, and systems change while they’re being repaired.
  5. The biology is broad. I’ve kept it at the level of principles. The real mechanisms are more varied and specific than these sketches.

How I use it in research

This memo adds five questions to the four in Memo 05.

  • Placement: Is this thing harmful, or is it in the wrong place, scale or stage? What used to keep it in balance?
  • Standing down: What protection is still switched on? What threat was it set for, and what signal would tell it the threat has passed?
  • Incentives: Whose success depends on the problem continuing? What would make recovery count as their win too?
  • Channels: Where is a resource failing to arrive because its channel is blocked? Is there a direct route for now?
  • Timing: When does repair cost the most, and who pays? Who judges whether it’s working, and how early? (Donella Meadows counts the length of delays, relative to how fast a system changes, among her leverage points [Meadows, 1999].)

A landscape designed to improve its ecosystem is a good test of all five. Before it’s built, protection takes the form of review: permits, standards and sign-offs, many of them set after real failures. After it’s built, the risk moves into the first years, when the planting is still rooting and looks least like the promise. Who is paid to look after it, and how? Who decides whether it’s working, and when? Who would gain if it needed them for longer? The answers decide whether a well-designed site reaches the year it can mostly look after itself.

Evidence status

This is an exploratory argument, not an empirical study. It draws on the frame in Memo 05, general biology, one documentary, and my own observation, and none of it has been reviewed by biologists or systems scientists.

What would change my mind: finding that systems recover as well from single interventions as from combined ones, or that tying protectors’ rewards to whole-system health makes no difference to how long protection stays on.
