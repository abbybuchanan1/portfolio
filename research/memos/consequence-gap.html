Research Memo 04

The Consequence Gap

An AI essay that slowly becomes an essay about ourselves.

Abby BuchananJuly 2026 · Updated November 2026Working memo · Provisional
Abstract

Most conversations about AI swing between extremes: it will save the world, or it will end it. This memo argues that a more revealing question is about the limits of human perception. Drawing on evolutionary biology, history, leadership, and personal experience, it looks at the gap between the consequences humans evolved to notice and the consequences modern technologies create, and what happens when our instincts to adapt and protect meet systems that play out across generations, continents, and whole ecosystems.

I’ve noticed that almost every conversation about AI turns into a conversation about who we believe we’re protecting: jobs, innovation, national security, shareholders, copyright, children, the environment, future generations. Underneath the arguments about technology is a quieter question. Who belongs inside our circle of protection?

When I meet a complex problem, my mind rarely produces a single opinion. It assembles a panel. I’ve learned to trust it, because if I stay with the conversation long enough, a different question usually shows up.

The ethicist questions whether anything is truly out of our hands while humans are still the ones making the decisions. “It’s inevitable” feels lazy at best and coercive at worst. The pragmatist assumes AI is here whether we’re ready or not. The idealist looks for ways to steer it toward the flourishing of people and the living systems around them. The realist sees real gains in productivity, analysis, and synthesis, even as unintended consequences pile up behind them. Then the researcher notices that AI rarely eliminates work. More often it moves it somewhere else.

The steward thinks about the people leading organizations through this. They have to weigh employees, customers, investors, regulators, and boards whose priorities rarely line up. Leadership starts to look less like choosing between right and wrong and more like choosing between competing responsibilities whose consequences arrive on different timelines. Moving too slowly is a risk, and so is moving too fast. I don’t envy that position. The pessimist quietly points out that we are speeding toward consequences we can’t yet understand.

Then the historian clears her throat. History is full of technologies that spread faster than our understanding of them: fire, agriculture, the printing press, industry, fossil fuels, the internet, social media. Each expanded what people could do, and only later did we start asking what it was doing to us. The hard part has rarely been inventing the capability. It’s been understanding the systems the capability creates. In the frame I later set out in Memo 05, a new capability quietly reshapes a system’s boundaries, where its resources flow, how it notices harm, and the roles its parts play, usually before anyone is watching for it.

The longer I sit with AI, the less I care whether it’s good or bad. The more interesting question is whether humans evolved to perceive consequences at the scale our technologies now produce them. What happens when instincts built for immediate, local consequences meet technologies whose consequences are delayed, spread out, and mostly invisible? And it isn’t only harm we struggle to see. The same gap hides slow good: a forest planted for people who won’t be born for a century, or a landscape designed to look after itself long after the people who made it are gone. Thinking at that scale is hard for us in both directions.

The biologist leans in. Our ancestors didn’t need to think fifty years ahead or across the globe to survive. They needed to spot immediate threats, protect the people closest to them, and find enough food to get through another season. Those instincts worked remarkably well; we’re the proof. A pizza or a bowl of ice cream would have been an evolutionary jackpot, which may be why we struggle to find the off switch. We’ve changed our environment much faster than we’ve changed ourselves. Our instincts reward what helps us here and now. Many of the systems we’ve built need us to care about what happens far away and much later. That tension may define this century.

Once survival was more reliable, knowledge became the next advantage. It reduced uncertainty and helped families and communities adjust when conditions changed. People who learned, adjusted, and experimented were more likely to come through hard times.

AI speaks straight to those instincts. It offers access to an almost unfathomable amount of quickly synthesized information, and it promises less effort, more efficiency, and help navigating a complicated world. For many people it doesn’t just feel useful. It has become necessary. Companies hear they must adopt AI or be left behind. Workers hear they must learn it or become obsolete. Some have watched colleagues lose jobs as organizations restructure around it, and others worry they’re next. The same technology can feel like both the lifeboat and the storm. The stakes are different from our ancestors’, but income, relevance, and employability can still feel tied to survival. No wonder AI is so compelling.

Then another voice joins the conversation: the mother. In my field, AI is already part of the work, and I expect that to grow. Using these tools may help me stay employable, earn more, work more efficiently, and provide for my children. That feels like protection. But I can’t shake an uncomfortable question. What if I’m confusing immediate security with long-term survival? If I use AI to support my family today while taking part, even in a tiny way, in systems that use enormous amounts of energy and water, speed up climate change, or widen inequality, have I actually protected my children? Or have I optimized for the next five years instead of the next fifty?

History is full of that paradox. Wars are fought to protect nations, yet cities burn. Forests are cleared to feed communities, and ecosystems collapse. Parents work longer hours to build security and lose the time they hoped to have with their children. Again and again, people act to protect the ones they love, and those same actions change the conditions their loved ones depend on, often without anyone noticing. That isn’t necessarily selfishness or short-sightedness. Our instincts may simply have been built for a world where cause and effect were visible, local, and immediate. Technology has stretched cause and effect across continents and generations. Decisions made in one room may not show their consequences for decades, and the people who bear them may be people we’ll never meet.

That makes resistance more complicated than it’s usually portrayed. People resist new technologies because they dislike change, because their identity or livelihood is threatened, because of moral, religious, or political convictions, because they see harms others are minimizing, or because caution is its own way of adapting. Sometimes they’re right. History has examples of each, which makes it very hard, in the moment, to tell resistance to progress from resistance to preventable harm.

That uncertainty keeps drawing me back. I don’t think I have the answers, but I do think we’re asking the wrong question. Instead of asking whether AI will save us or ruin us, I’d ask: what consequences are we able to perceive, and which ones do our instincts reliably overlook? What we perceive shapes what we choose to protect.

Evolution built us to protect our own. That instinct is beautiful, but it’s also incomplete. The next challenge may be widening it instead of abandoning it: placing the most vulnerable at the center of the circle instead of the edge. That means children, future generations, communities with the least power over the systems shaping their lives, and the ecosystems and non-human life our own survival depends on. Protecting them may be another way of protecting ourselves, fifty years from now.

Every generation draws a circle around the lives it considers worth protecting. Sometimes the circle grows, and sometimes it shrinks. The technologies we build and the systems we adopt show where we’ve drawn it. Each generation also gets the chance to draw it a little wider than the last. I wonder if that’s what this moment is asking of us.
