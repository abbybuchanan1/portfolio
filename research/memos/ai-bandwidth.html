Research Memo 02

The AI Divide Is About Bandwidth

How AI changes capacity more than capability

Abby BuchananJuly 2026 · Updated November 2026Working memo · Provisional
Abstract

AI is often described as a productivity tool. I think it changes something more basic: how much cognitive bandwidth people have to work with. This memo looks at why the real divide in AI adoption may have less to do with intelligence than with the conditions people are asked to learn under.

There’s a growing assumption that AI will divide people by who understands it first: who knows how to use it, who writes better prompts, who can keep up. That doesn’t quite match what I’m seeing.

To me the divide looks more like bandwidth, or what I call cognitive margin: the room a person has, on a given day, beyond what they already have to handle. Not everyone moves through the day with the same amount of it. Some people have room to experiment. They can spend an hour exploring a new tool without worrying whether it was the best use of their time, and they can sit with the awkwardness of learning something unfamiliar.

AI is usually described as saving time. I’d put it a little differently: it lowers the effort of turning thought into something usable. It helps organize ideas, draft messages, summarize information, and add structure where there wasn’t any. In the terms of my first memo, it pays part of the invisible tax. The philosopher Andy Clark would call it a cognitive extender: he has long argued that human thinking already reaches beyond the brain into notebooks, calculators, and other tools, and he sees generative AI as the newest layer of that (Clark, 2025). An extender doesn’t make people more capable, but it can change how much of their existing capability they bring into the room.

The catch is that getting that benefit takes margin too, and I think we underestimate that. Margin isn’t handed out at random. Economic pressure, workload, caregiving, paperwork, and the complexity of the systems people have to navigate all use it up. Under those conditions a new tool can feel less like an opportunity and more like one more thing asking for attention. Stress narrows attention, makes experimenting more expensive, and pulls decisions toward the short term. The people who could gain the most from extra cognitive support may have the least room to take it up.

These are the questions I’m most interested in now: Does AI make it easier for different kinds of minds to contribute? Does it lower the translation cost of knowledge work? Or does it mostly speed up the people who already had room to adapt, and widen the gap between those with margin and those without? Those feel like research questions worth pursuing.

Evidence status

This memo is an exploratory argument, not a completed study. It develops questions and distinctions for further research; factual and causal claims need independent sourcing before they’re treated as findings.
