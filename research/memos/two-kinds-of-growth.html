Research Memo 05

Two Kinds of Growth

Why some systems grow by extraction and others by development

Abby BuchananNovember 2026Working memo · Provisional
Abstract

Living systems at every scale have to solve the same four problems: where their boundaries are, how resources move, how harm is noticed and corrected, and how parts take on different roles. When those functions keep correcting themselves, a system matures. When they are captured or suppressed, a system can keep growing while losing its health. This memo sets out that frame, the case that prompted it, where it breaks, and how I use it in research.

Some growth builds a living system up. Some growth feeds on it.

Terms

I use these words the same way throughout this memo and the next.

Growth by extraction (extractive growth): depriving one living thing of vital resources to feed another. The system can keep getting bigger while the part it draws from weakens.

Growth by development (developmental growth): the parts become stronger and more capable together, because each gets the conditions and resources it needs.

Self-correction: a system’s own ability to notice harm and adjust, the way a body keeps its temperature steady. Not rules or permits imposed from outside.

The four functions: boundaries, resource flow, feedback and differentiation. The four things every living system has to manage, set out below.

Where this began

A couple of years ago, I tried to understand why colonization starts, why it persists, and why its effects outlast the end of formal empire. I built a matrix of the conditions involved: what makes expansion profitable, what lets power travel, what keeps control in place at a distance, and what makes domination feel justified.

Partway through, I noticed something I didn’t expect. The conditions mapped closely onto how a chronic infection establishes itself in a body. I’m not an immunologist; my understanding of these mechanisms is general.

An infection enters where there is something to take (Marchetti et al., 2020). It spreads through efficient transmission. It uses the host’s own cells to do its work (Chinchar, 1999). Some pathogens don’t overpower the immune system; they quiet the signals that would trigger a response (Taylor and Mossman, 2013). And after it is cleared, the body usually carries memory, and sometimes scar tissue (LibreTexts; AMBOSS).

The parallel is not only metaphorical. The environmental historian Alfred Crosby argued that European expansion succeeded as much through the animals, plants and pathogens that traveled with colonists as through military force (Crosby, 2015).

My interest here is structural. The same pattern seemed to recur at different scales, often in both human and non-human life, and I wanted to know why.

The mapping

This is the correspondence that surprised me. Each row is a structural parallel: a similar move made by a different kind of system.

The shared moves are the point. They describe how any system can be captured from within, whether a body, a society or an organization.

The four functions

Every complex adaptive system, whether a cell, a body, an institution or a society, has to manage four things.

The four functions

BoundariesWhat is the entity, where does it begin and end, and what may enter or leave?Skin, membranes, immune recognitionBorders, jurisdiction, consentInvasion, or dissolution
Resource flowWhere does energy go, and who receives it?Blood supply, nutrientsTaxes, labor, data, attentionExtraction that drains the whole

When these stay in balance, systems grow by development. When they are distorted, three patterns appear.

Three ways growth goes wrong, and one way it goes right

Invasion. Something from outside crosses a boundary and takes resources. Sometimes the extraction is immediate and obvious. Sometimes the invader evades detection long enough to be drawing resources before anyone knows it’s there. Once it’s noticed, one of two things happens: it is cleared and the system recovers, or it holds on through force and dominance, and the next pattern begins.

Chronic embedding. The invasion persists, and the system adapts to it. Extraction becomes normal, feedback is blunted, and dependency forms. The system survives, but in a weakened, altered form. This is the closest structural parallel I found to colonization.

Runaway growth. Growth comes from inside but detaches from purpose. Stop signals are ignored and one part takes resources at the expense of the whole. In institutions, this looks like a growth metric that overrides wellbeing, or power that no longer answers to anyone.

Development. Not all expansion is a problem. The clearest model I know of healthy growth is fetal development, because it shows the difference between growth that creates life and growth that consumes it. Developmental growth has six features:

  1. Integration by consent. Parts join through exchange, not capture.
  2. Boundary organs. Something like a placenta manages what crosses between systems, in both directions.
  3. Differentiation. Parts become more different from each other, not more alike.
  4. Finite stages. Growth happens in phases, each with its own stop signal.
  5. Planned separation. The relationship is designed to end, or change form, from the start.
  6. Increasing autonomy. The growing part needs less from its host over time, not more.

Development is expansion that keeps its feedback intact and moves toward independence.

The difference between extractive and developmental growth isn’t size or speed. It is whether the four functions keep correcting themselves as the system grows.

Growth is the easy part

When I looked at why large systems of domination eventually failed, the pattern was consistent. They rarely failed at expansion. They failed at sustaining what they had built, when conditions changed and the systems that held everything together either could not, or would not, adapt. The contrast is immunity: a body that meets a threat, absorbs it and survives learns to resist it next time. Systems that last have that same capacity to learn from what they take in.

The same is true of good things. A plan can be approved, a product launched, a landscape planted. Whether it lasts depends on the slower, less visible work of upkeep: who maintains it, who notices when it’s failing, and whether anyone can change course.

A holistic picture of growth is rarely linear. A tree’s visible height depends on roots and branches spreading outward through countless small exchanges. We track GDP the same way: one rising line that hides billions of individual choices, successes, failures and compromises. Some years the tree doesn’t get taller, but the roots have found their way around underground boulders.

Repair is the subject of a companion memo, Memo 06, Protection and Repair: why systems can stay unwell after a threat has passed, and what recovery takes. Its short version: repair is not reversal. Swapping who is in charge without addressing the extractive system itself keeps the same logic running; repair restores the functions themselves.

What this is not

Analogies across scales are powerful and easy to misuse, so I hold this one to six limits.

  1. Analogy, not equivalence. Societies are not organisms. The claim is that similar structural dynamics recur, not that the systems are the same.
  2. People are not pathology. These are descriptions of system states, never of groups of people. Disease language has a long history of being turned against people, and this frame must not add to it. Infection is not evil. Cancer is not immoral. Disease is not punishment. These are system states.
  3. Nothing here is inevitable. Infection language can imply that outcomes are natural or unavoidable. Colonization was made of human choices, incentives and designs, and so is any repair.
  4. Development is not always gentle. Healthy growth also involves rupture, risk and loss. The frame shouldn’t romanticize it.
  5. Not everything is extraction. Before calling a pattern extractive, I ask four questions. Was a boundary crossed without consent? Is feedback being suppressed? Are resources flowing one way? Is there growth with no exit? If the answers are no, it isn’t this pattern.
  6. Harm often comes from misplacement, not malice. Something can help in one place and harm in another. Before calling anything bad in itself, I ask whether it’s in the wrong place, at the wrong scale or at the wrong time.

Others have found pieces of this

Comparing societies to bodies isn’t new; the idea of the “body politic” is centuries old. What I’m offering is a specific mapping of how a system is captured from within, and four functions to test it against. I arrived at this frame on my own, and then found that others had mapped parts of it with far more evidence. Where I draw on other people’s work, I name them.

Elinor Ostrom’s studies of long-lasting commons, such as forests, fisheries and irrigation systems, found shared design principles. They include clearly defined boundaries, rules fitted to local conditions, and monitoring by people accountable to the users (Ostrom, 1990). Those are boundaries, resource flow and feedback, observed in real institutions over generations.

Donella Meadows ranked leverage points: places where, in her words, “a small shift in one thing can produce big changes in everything.” In her list, adjusting numbers such as subsidies and standards is among the weakest interventions, while changing information flows, rules and goals reaches much deeper (Meadows, 1999). In this frame’s terms, repairing feedback does more than tuning resources.

How I use it in research

The frame gives me four questions to ask of any product, service, institution or place I study.

  • Boundaries: Who decides? Who can enter, and who can leave? How do we know when someone has entered, and when they’ve gone? Is consent clear?
  • Resource flow: Who pays, in money, time, attention or data, and who benefits? Do the two line up?
  • Feedback: When something goes wrong, who notices first? Does that signal reach anyone who can act?
  • Differentiation: Is there more than one way to succeed? Can different people play different roles? How much capacity exists to manage the risks and unknowns that experimenting with new roles inevitably brings?

It also connects to my earlier memo, The Invisible Tax. The effort people spend translating their needs and ideas into forms a system accepts is a resource-flow question: someone is paying that cost, and it is usually not the system. I reached the same idea from the opposite direction while working on this frame: one of the quieter effects of domination is that people learn to translate themselves into someone else’s categories in order to be legible. Two unrelated lines of inquiry meeting at the same point is part of why I think it’s worth testing.

An example I’m working on now: a landscape designed to improve its ecosystem still has to pass through many people before it is built and established. Decision rights shift at each handoff. Timing matters as much as handoffs. Donella Meadows counts the length of delays, relative to how fast a system changes, among her leverage points. A landscape changes with the seasons, so a permit held up for a few weeks could push planting past its window and cost the site a year of growth. The cost of the first years of maintenance often falls on one party while the benefits reach others later. Failures in the planting may never reach the people who designed it. And each party needs a clear role in a plan that runs for years. All four functions decide whether a good design survives contact with the people who carry it out.

Evidence status

This is an exploratory argument, not an empirical study. It grew from a personal analytical exercise that has not been reviewed by historians, immunologists or systems scientists. The historical claims I relied on most are sourced below; others in my original notes were not, and I have left them out. I also dropped a numerical scoring of historical empires, because its method wasn’t rigorous enough to publish.

How I tested it: I built the original framework from historical cases, then tried to break it. I looked for hard cases the framework should have predicted and didn’t, and asked what had to be true for it to survive. Where it failed, I revised it; where a single factor seemed to explain too much, I checked whether it only mattered in combination with others. The section “What this is not” comes directly from that process.

I’ve kept the biology at the level of broad principles. A closer look at the actual mechanisms of infection, immunity and development would likely sharpen the mapping, and change parts of it. That’s work I’d want to do with people who study those systems.

What would change my mind: finding systems that grow healthily for long periods with weak feedback, or extractive systems that persist with strong, independent feedback.
