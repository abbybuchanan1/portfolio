Research Memo 03

The Opportunity Gap

Who actually benefits when AI increases capacity

Abby BuchananJuly 2026 · Updated November 2026Working memo · Provisional
Abstract

AI can lower the effort it takes to organize information, communicate ideas, and find a way through complex systems. Lower effort isn’t the same as more opportunity. This memo looks at why the benefits of AI land unevenly, and why its long-term effect may depend as much on how institutions are designed as on the technology.

Memo 02 asked who has the bandwidth to adopt AI. This memo asks what happens next: when AI does save someone time and effort, who gets to keep it?

If someone finishes a task in half the time because AI helped organize their thinking or draft a first pass, something of value has been created. Sometimes it’s clear who benefits: a student who finally understands a concept after asking for it explained three different ways, a parent getting back an hour of mental energy, someone completing a process that used to feel impossible. In those cases AI expands what feels possible, not only how much gets done.

Workplaces don’t always treat saved effort that way. Often the time saved doesn’t stay with the person who saved it. It becomes the new baseline. Expectations rise, more work fills the space, and the person becomes more productive without becoming any less burdened.

That makes me think the resource AI creates may be less about time than about cognitive margin, the same room Memo 02 called bandwidth: room to pause before responding, to think instead of react, to make connections instead of just keeping up. That margin has always been unevenly shared. People with financial stability, flexible schedules, supportive workplaces, and strong networks tend to have more of it. People carrying economic pressure, caregiving, disability, burnout, or complicated systems tend to have less.

AI could shift that. It can lower the translation work modern systems require, which I wrote about in The Invisible Tax. But nothing guarantees it will. If employers and institutions absorb the capacity AI creates and simply ask for more, the technology may raise efficiency without widening opportunity. If more of that capacity stays with individuals rather than institutions, they may have more room to learn, to recover, and to contribute in ways they couldn’t before. And if AI mostly raises the standard for people already succeeding inside a system, it widens the gap for people who weren’t in it to begin with: the bar to get in keeps rising while they’re still outside.

So part of the future of AI is a design question: who benefits from what it makes possible? I’m interested in designing systems that narrow that gap instead of widening it.

Evidence status

This memo is an exploratory argument, not a completed study. It develops questions and distinctions for further research; factual and causal claims need independent sourcing before they’re treated as findings.
