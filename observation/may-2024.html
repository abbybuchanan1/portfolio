Poem · 2024

May, 2024

Full text, offered as optional context for the observation practice.

It is the month the thirteen-year and seventeen-year cycles synchronize. Cicada broods XIII and XIX co-emerge from the ground for the first time in 221 years. Seven states across the Midwest and Southeast will reverberate with cicada song until mid-June, when the mothers lay their eggs in the ends of tree branches. The newly minted nymphs hatch, free-fall to the roots, feast on sap, then follow their ancestral path down deep into the earth: to burrow, incubate, wait. It is the month I sit in the back of a cab and wonder if I can write a poem after thirty years. Rafah is on fire. I rage, make calls, watch the videos to the end until my cheeks burn, knowing it is both the most and the least I can do. She gets her first loose tooth. He gets his first pimple. It is the month I see my mother look genuinely happy in a photo for the first time. When the largest solar storm since 2003 erupts above us, we wake the kids at midnight, run down to the riverbank, pajamas and bare feet, to gawk at the aurora. They are hard to see with the naked eye, but strangely brilliant through my iPhone’s lens: pink, green, gold. We sit on a bench in front of Sue, the world’s largest, most complete T-rex fossil. We hold up photos of chicken skeletons, then human skeletons, comparing them to Sue’s. None are wildly different from the other. We wonder if apex predators get a bad rap. In a quiet corner of the museum’s basement we see a mummy with our own eyes. She is smaller than I anticipated, not much bigger than a large child. I fall in love with the wide gold wings gilding her chest. I wonder how her afterlife is going. And whether it is disgust or fascination rippling through my belly when I see her partially exposed maxilla. It is not the month we finish construction on our house. But it is the month my brother, intrepid archaeologist, hauls away 2,500 pounds of trash and debris, unearthing, presenting: the yard. The yard where my mother plants flowers. Where we gather on Mother’s Day with old and new loves. My Dear One hears a story stir deep underground, a story buried with our friend after the car accident in 2000. She sings it back to the surface with the cicadas, sings it with her whole chest to the cameras. Then sings him back to sleep so he can finally rest. It is the month I sit in the clinic every week, IV in my left arm, as iron is pumped back into my watery blood. It is the month I exhume imprints of both abuser and abused embedded in my body. Gold in my veins when the light hits just right. It is the month I remember I love sex. The month I bleed with the full moon. He and I see a prick of light pierce our familiar dark. Maybe we have been in a swelling cocoon all along, not a tomb as we feared. May is the month my nine-year-old, blood of my blood, takes my hand unprompted as we walk through the park. The month my five-year-old, bone of my bone, falls asleep on my chest, her head heavy on my sternum, feet reaching all the way to the center of my tibias. (How?) It is the month I realize human ashes, viewed under a microscope, resemble nebulae seen through a telescope. The month the nymph surrenders. The mother emerges. The month I finally wade out into the river, offering my exhausted attempts at control to her swift current. At least for today. By Abby Buchanan (2024)
